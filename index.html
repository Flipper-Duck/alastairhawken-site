Cut away the labels

Some labels were never yours to carry.

A label can describe a moment. It should never be allowed to define a life.

As a child, Alastair did not pass the 11+ grammar school entrance exam. The word “failure” entered his story early and he carried it for around 30 years before learning that a label imposed by somebody else does not have to become an identity.

In 2020, during the breakdown of a 20-year marriage, the word “failure” appeared repeatedly in the divorce petition. By then Alastair had learned something he had not understood as a child: other people are allowed to have an opinion about you. They are not entitled to define your value.

Disinherit negative labels.

Labels can come from parents, partners, schools, employers, relationships, failures and our own inner voice. Some were once useful. Some were never true. Some have simply expired.

The work is about recognising which labels are no longer fit for purpose, cutting them away and refusing to devalue yourself because somebody else failed to see your value.

Stop letting other people bring you down. Believe in yourself. Build your future from evidence, not from somebody else’s verdict.

My story

I did not learn resilience from a textbook.

My career began in broadcasting. I went on to work across national and international television, including BBC News, ITV and the launch of Channel 5. Live television taught me how to make decisions under pressure, recover quickly when something goes wrong and keep moving when there is no time for perfect certainty.

I then moved into entrepreneurship. I founded businesses, raised investment, built teams, opened locations and learned what responsibility feels like when other people's livelihoods depend on the decisions you make.

In 2005 a company I had built collapsed. I lost the business, lost my home and reached the lowest point of my life. I attempted to take my own life. I survived, rebuilt and later bought the business back.

Those experiences are not a list of wounds. They are the raw material from which perspective, judgement and resilience are built.

Peaks and troughs

Life is a waveform, not a straight line.

The troughs are not empty space. They are where some of the most important learning happens.

Alastair is a great believer in life behaving like a waveform: peaks, troughs, recovery, momentum and change. We celebrate the peaks because they feel good, but the troughs often do more of the shaping.

His view is that the difficult chapters can become tools of life. The value does not come from suffering for its own sake. It comes from processing what happened, learning what it revealed and deciding what you will carry forward.

Embrace the battle scars. Learn from the wake they leave behind. Then use that lived experience to build what comes next.

The aim is not to become the person you were before the setback. It is to become a more experienced, more self-aware and more intentional version of yourself.

When life strips things away

Your circumstances can collapse without your value collapsing with them.

When Alastair’s business collapsed in 2005 he lost the company, lost his home and, for a period, ended up sleeping in the back of his car. The external signs of success disappeared very quickly.

Years later, the breakdown of a long marriage brought another kind of loss. In the years that followed he experienced painful family estrangement and a serious incident of violence within that family conflict that was witnessed by one of his sons.

Those experiences reinforced a principle at the heart of his speaking: you cannot always control the behaviour of other people, the circumstances around you or the labels they try to attach to you. You can decide what meaning you give those experiences and what you build from them.

Be the best living expression of yourself.

Do not reduce your worth because another person cannot see it. Do not confuse what happened to you with who you are. Do not allow a bad chapter to become the title of the whole book.

Confidence is not pretending the scars are not there. It is being able to look at them, understand what they taught you and move forward without shame.

The future is not built by erasing the past. It is built by using it well.

Value is bigger than money

Having money does not necessarily make you rich.

Lived experience makes you rich too. The currency is simply different.

In 2021 Alastair donated stem cells to a complete stranger whose life depended on a transplant. He has donated blood and platelets for around 30 years.

That experience reinforced a belief that runs through his speaking: human value is not measured only in money, titles, grades, status or whether somebody else approves of you.

Nothing is automatically off limits

Sometimes the box is the thing constricting us.

Alastair built Hawkens Gingerbread from nothing into a business stocked in around 6,500 stores across the globe. The brand featured on three prime-time BBC One programmes, on ITV1 and on national radio, with Alastair speaking publicly about business and entrepreneurship.

In 2025 he established a pioneering UK crop of food-grade ginger. The point was not simply the crop. It was the mindset behind attempting something that conventional thinking said was unlikely.

Not everything is possible. But far more is possible than we often assume. Within reason, the first question should not be “Why can't I?” but “What would have to be true for this to work?”

What people take away from Alastair's work

Not just a good feeling. A different way of seeing themselves and what comes next.

  • Separate an event from an identity. Learn to say “that failed” without turning it into “I am a failure”.
  • Cut away labels that are no longer fit for purpose. Recognise inherited, imposed and self created labels that have been allowed to outlive their usefulness.
  • Stop outsourcing your value. Understand that another person's opinion, rejection or judgement is not a valuation certificate.
  • Revalue lived experience. See difficult chapters as a source of judgement, adaptability, perspective and resilience rather than wasted time.
  • Embrace the battle scars. Use what life has left in its wake as material for the future rather than evidence against yourself.
  • Build confidence from evidence. Ground self belief in what you have already survived, learned, contributed and achieved.
  • Understand the waveform of life. Expect peaks and troughs, process the lessons in both, and stop treating every trough as proof that life has gone wrong.
  • Challenge the box around you. Ask what might become possible if an old assumption is removed.
  • Become the best living expression of yourself. Make choices that reflect your values rather than somebody else's label for you.
  • Take the next useful action. Move from rumination and self judgement into a concrete step and a direction you can act on.
